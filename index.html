Oshi Lab
Independent software studio · Switzerland

Oshi Lab is an independent studio that builds software for Apple platforms. It began with an encrypted messenger, and the discipline that project required — doing the work on the device, keeping as little as possible, and being specific about what the software cannot do — is what the studio has carried into everything since.

The work is not a category. A navigation app for European drivers, an adaptive training coach, a daily word game, a screen-capture tool, a rank tracker for other developers: these are separate products with separate audiences, separate roadmaps and separate problems, and they are built that way. What they have in common is a studio, not a template.

OSHI

The project the studio is named after: an encrypted messenger with no account, no phone number and no address book upload. WayCar

Navigation written for European driving rather than adapted to it: national vignettes announced before the border, tolls and fuel costed into the route so the “cheapest” option is a number in euros, and electric-charge planning based on the consumption the car actually records rather than the manufacturer’s figure. French first, because that is who it was written for.

Trainora

A training coach that starts from the workouts already sitting in Apple Health, whatever recorded them, and builds a week from your own volume. Every prescribed session carries the reason it was prescribed, and the model reports how confident it is, because a fortnight of history is not a season of it.

Orakl

A decision simulator. You describe what you are weighing; a swarm of simulated agents seeded with deliberately opposing priors argues it out on the device, and you get the strongest case on each side plus a measure of how divided the swarm was. It runs in airplane mode, which is the only reason it is reasonable to type a real decision into it.

Lexical

Two word puzzles a day, one French and one English, solved by meaning rather than by letters: you are told how close a guess is semantically, not which characters were right. The French and English puzzles use different data because the two languages are not translations of one another.

RoamGuard

A mobile-data monitor that keeps home and roaming as two separate pools and warns before an overage rather than reporting one. AdScope

A tool for other independent developers: App Store keyword ranks measured across storefronts and kept as history, alongside read-only Apple Search Ads reporting. Written because the studio needed it, and useful mainly to people with the same problem.

How the studio works

  1. One product, one owner, one problemEach app is owned end to end rather than produced from a house template. They do not share a design system, a roadmap or a release train, and the sites you reach from here are deliberately not built alike either.
  2. Do the work on the device where the device can do itThis is a preference, not a rule we pretend is universal. A messenger needs a relay; a ride-hailing app needs a server and an account; a land-registry tool needs a database. Where an app genuinely does not need one — a game, a puzzle, a tracker — it does not get one, and it keeps working on a plane.
  3. Publish the limits, not only the featuresEvery product page here has a section on what the software cannot do and why, written before the marketing copy. Most of the time the reason is a platform boundary rather than a missing feature, and saying so is more useful than implying it will arrive later.
  4. Ship in the user’s languageInterface text lives in string catalogues, not in the code, and most of the apps ship in more than thirty languages. Several were written in French first and translated outward rather than the other way round.
  5. Answer the emailThere is one address for support, privacy requests and press, and it is read by the people who wrote the software.
